Section 01

Quantum Concepts — What They Mean in Physics

Three core concepts from quantum mechanics appear throughout this guide. Here is precisely what each means in actual physics — before any discussion of how their mathematics is borrowed for IR.

QUANTUM PHYSICS
⚛ Superposition
A quantum particle exists in multiple states simultaneously until measured. A qubit is both 0 and 1 at the same time — not alternating, not blended, but genuinely both. Measurement collapses it to one definite value. Schrödinger's cat: alive and dead inside the box until you open it.
QUANTUM PHYSICS
〰 Interference
Quantum particles behave like waves. Two waves overlap and either add (constructive — brighter) or cancel (destructive — darkness). In the double-slit experiment, an electron creates an interference pattern as if it went through both slits simultaneously — even when fired one at a time.
QUANTUM PHYSICS
🔗 Entanglement
Two particles become correlated so measuring one instantly determines the other's state, regardless of distance. Einstein called it "spooky action at a distance." The joint state cannot be written as two independent states — it is a fundamentally non-classical correlation.
QUANTUM PHYSICS
📐 Measurement
Observing a quantum system disturbs it. Before measurement: superposition of states. After: collapses to one outcome with probability = |amplitude|². This squaring of amplitude to get probability is the most fundamental quantum law — and the operation quantum-inspired IR borrows most directly.
Section 02

Real-Life Uses of Quantum Concepts

Where superposition, interference, and entanglement are actually exploited in practice — not as inspiration, but as the physical mechanism delivering value.

Superposition

💊
DRUG DISCOVERY
Molecular simulation
A molecule's electrons exist in superposition of energy states. Quantum computers simulate this directly — classically you need 2^N parameters for N electrons. IBM and Google already simulate small molecules (H₂, LiH, iron-sulfur clusters) relevant to drug design and fertilizer chemistry.
🔐
CRYPTOGRAPHY
Shor's algorithm
A quantum computer in superposition explores all possible factors simultaneously. Shor's algorithm breaks RSA encryption exponentially faster than any classical method. Governments are already transitioning to post-quantum cryptography — the threat is real even if hardware isn't ready yet.
🎲
SECURITY
Quantum random numbers
Quantum measurement outcomes are genuinely random — physically impossible to predict. Classical computers only produce pseudorandom numbers. ID Quantique's quantum RNG chips are already in Samsung Galaxy phones and banking security systems. The one quantum application already on consumer devices.

Interference

🔬
PRECISION MEASUREMENT
Atom interferometry
Matter waves of atoms are split, sent along different paths, then recombined. The interference pattern reveals how paths differed with extreme precision — detecting gravitational fields, mapping underground structure. Used in navigation that doesn't need GPS and in oil/mineral exploration.
🌊
SENSING
LIGO gravitational waves
LIGO uses laser interferometry to detect gravitational waves from black hole collisions billions of light-years away. Two laser beams travel 4km arms and recombine — a wave slightly stretches one arm, changing the interference pattern. Sensitivity: 1/10,000th the diameter of a proton.
🖥
COMPUTING
Grover's search speedup
Quantum algorithms like Grover's use interference to amplify correct answers and suppress wrong ones. Each iteration: correct answer grows via constructive interference, wrong answers cancel via destructive interference. This gives O(√N) vs classical O(N). The speedup comes entirely from interference.

Entanglement

🛡
COMMUNICATION
Quantum key distribution
Entangled photons distribute encryption keys where eavesdropping is physically detectable — it disturbs the entanglement. China operates a 2000km quantum communication backbone (Beijing–Shanghai, 2017). Toshiba sells commercial QKD systems. EU's EuroQCI quantum internet project is ongoing.
⚗️
CHEMISTRY
Quantum chemistry
Electrons in molecules are entangled — you cannot describe each independently without losing correlation information. Simulating this entanglement is what requires quantum computers. Classical computers cannot efficiently track all electron-electron correlations in large molecules. The strongest near-term quantum use case.
📡
NETWORKING
Quantum repeaters
Long-distance quantum networks need repeaters that preserve entanglement without measuring it (measurement destroys it). Quantum repeaters use entanglement swapping — entangling particles that never directly interacted via a third particle. Lab demonstrations already over 100km. Essential for a future quantum internet.
Important: None of these apply to information retrieval. Quantum-inspired IR borrows the mathematics of quantum mechanics — Hilbert space, density matrices, complex amplitudes — not the physical phenomena. Superposition of qubits, quantum entanglement, wave function collapse: none happen in any IR pipeline running on classical hardware.
Section 03

Why Not Use Real Quantum Hardware?

Five hard blockers make quantum hardware impossible for on-device mobile IR — now and for the foreseeable future.

Blocker 1 — QRAM Does Not Exist
// To run IR on quantum hardware, you must load embeddings into quantum memory (QRAM) classical RAM: load N docs → O(N) time — trivial QRAM promise: load N docs → O(log N) time QRAM reality: does not exist at any useful scale best lab demos: ~10 qubits in controlled conditions your corpus: millions of docs, 384-dim embeddings
Blocker 2 — Nowhere Near Enough Qubits
// Qubit requirement for your pipeline encode one 384-dim float32 vector: 384 × 32 = 12,288 high-quality qubits per document for 100,000 docs: 100,000 × 12,288 = 1.2 billion qubits needed best quantum computers today (2025): IBM Heron: ~133 qubits · Google Willow: ~105 qubits gap: 10 million× too few
Blocker 3 — Decoherence
// Qubits lose quantum state extremely quickly qubit coherence time: 100–500 microseconds time needed for IR query: milliseconds to seconds → state collapses before search finishes fix: error correction → 1,000 physical qubits per logical qubit → 1.2 billion × 1,000 = 1.2 trillion physical qubits needed
Blocker 4 — Grover's Speedup Cancels Out
// The algorithmic gain disappears on real hardware Grover's algorithm: O(√N) — 1,000× fewer ops for N=1M docs BUT: classical op speed: ~1 nanosecond quantum op speed: ~1 microsecond (1,000× slower per op) net speedup: 1,000 fewer ops × 1,000× slower each = 1× — no actual gain
Blocker 5 — Physically Incompatible With Mobile
// Quantum computer requirements vs your target quantum computer needs: your phone: ────────────────────────────── ───────────────────── cooling to -273°C ambient ~20°C room-sized cryogenic fridge fits in a pocket EM isolation chamber held next to your head specialized control electronics battery powered physically incompatible — quantum mobile: impossible // Server-side quantum IR: 2040s at earliest. Mobile: never.
Section 04

Quantum-Inspired — Math vs Physics

Quantum-inspired IR uses the mathematical framework of quantum mechanics on entirely classical hardware. No qubits. No cryogenics. No quantum effects whatsoever.

QUANTUM PHYSICS

Uses actual quantum systems — qubits in superposition, entangled particles, wave function collapse.

Requires dilution refrigerators at near absolute zero, quantum optical systems.

Cannot run on classical computers.

Timeline for useful IR: 2040s on servers. Never on mobile.

QUANTUM-INSPIRED

Uses the mathematical language — Hilbert space, density matrices, complex numbers, unitary operators — on classical CPUs and NPUs.

Runs on your phone's existing chip. No special hardware.

Timeline: Implementable today. Density matrix needs zero retraining.

// What quantum-inspired IR borrows vs leaves behind BORROWED (runs on classical hardware): ✓ Hilbert space → complex inner product space, richer than Euclidean ✓ Density matrices → d×d matrix per doc, captures term correlations ✓ Complex inner product → ⟨q|d⟩ = Σ q_k* × d_k, phase-sensitive similarity ✓ Interference → contributions interact before squaring ✓ Measurement postulate → |z|² gives real non-negative probability score ✓ Mixed state → ρ = Σ pᵢ·|ψᵢ⟩⟨ψᵢ| for ambiguous queries LEFT BEHIND (requires actual quantum hardware): ✗ Physical superposition → qubits genuinely in multiple states ✗ Quantum entanglement → correlated collapse across physical particles ✗ Wave function collapse → physical measurement disturbing the system ✗ Quantum speedup → Grover, HHL — need QRAM + fault-tolerant qubits

Key insight: Interference between documents in QPRP, off-diagonal term co-occurrence in density matrices, phase-sensitive scoring in complex embeddings — all are classical mathematical operations that quantum physics happens to use. You could derive them from linear algebra and functional analysis with no mention of quantum mechanics. The quantum framework provides a unified, self-consistent language for operations classical IR had no natural way to express.

Section 05A · Implementation One

Density Matrices

What is a density matrix?
A classical bi-encoder represents each document as a vector — a single point in semantic space. A density matrix represents it as a matrix — a 2D structure capturing not just how much of each concept is present, but how concepts relate to each other within the document. Computed by a simple outer product: ρ = |v⟩⟨v| — multiply the vector by itself transposed.

Technical Terms

Hilbert Space
The mathematical space where quantum-inspired IR operates. Unlike ordinary Euclidean space (real-number vectors), Hilbert space allows complex-valued entries and defines a specific inner product rule: ⟨q|d⟩ = Σₖ q_k* × d_k. What matters for IR: Hilbert space lets us define probabilities as squared magnitudes of amplitudes — the fundamental quantum probability rule that density matrices and complex embeddings both use.
Ket |v⟩ and Bra ⟨v| — Quantum Notation for Vectors
|v⟩ (called "ket") is a column vector — the document or query embedding. ⟨v| (called "bra") is its row vector (transpose). The outer product |v⟩⟨v| multiplies a column vector by a row vector to produce a matrix. If v = [a, b, c], then |v⟩⟨v| is the 3×3 matrix where entry [i][j] = v[i] × v[j]. It is just a multiplication table of the vector with itself.
Density Matrix ρ (rho)
The matrix representation of a document or query. ρ is always square (d×d), symmetric (ρ[i][j] = ρ[j][i]), and positive semidefinite. Diagonal entries ρ[i][i] = v[i]² — how strongly dimension i is present. Off-diagonal entries ρ[i][j] = v[i]×v[j] — how strongly dimensions i and j co-occur in the document. This co-occurrence information is what classical vectors cannot represent.
Pure State vs Mixed State
Pure state: ρ = |v⟩⟨v| — one definite vector. You know exactly which direction the document/query points. Tr(ρ²) = 1.

Mixed state: ρ = Σᵢ pᵢ·|vᵢ⟩⟨vᵢ| — a weighted sum of multiple pure states where pᵢ are probabilities summing to 1. Used when there is genuine uncertainty about which state applies — e.g. "apple" could mean fruit or brand. Tr(ρ²) < 1.

The key advantage: classical always commits to one vector. Mixed state stays genuinely uncertain until user signal arrives.
Tr(ρ_q · ρ_doc) — The Scoring Function
Tr means "trace" — sum of diagonal entries. Tr(ρ_q · ρ_doc) equals elementwise multiply-then-sum: Σᵢⱼ ρ_q[i][j] × ρ_doc[i][j]. For pure states this equals cosine(q,doc)² — same ranking as classical. The power comes when the query is a mixed state: then Tr decomposes as Σᵢ pᵢ·Tr(ρ_sense_i·ρ_doc), scoring each sense independently. This is what classical cosine cannot do.

Critical fact: For pure state density matrices (ρ = |v⟩⟨v|), Tr(ρ_q · ρ_doc) always equals cosine(q,doc)². The ranking is identical to classical — just values squared. This is mathematically guaranteed. The genuine advantages are (1) mixed state queries for ambiguous terms and (2) QPRP inter-document interference for diversity — both shown below with full calculations.

Worked Example C — QPRP Inter-Document Diversity

Classical scores every doc against the query independently. QPRP additionally computes Tr(ρ_A · ρ_B) between result docs — high = redundant, low = diverse.

1
Compute inter-document interference Tr(ρ_i · ρ_j) for all pairs
// Using query "apple juice", same A/B/C from Example A // Relevance: B(0.8901) > A(0.7547) > C(0.0236) Tr(ρ_A · ρ_B): (food,food): 0.8265 × 0.0989 = 0.0818 (food,drink): 0.3673 × 0.2967 = 0.1089 (drink,food): 0.3673 × 0.2967 = 0.1089 (drink,drink): 0.1633 × 0.8901 = 0.1453 (others near 0) total = 0.4593 ← substantial overlap — A and B are similar docs! 2
QPRP reranking — select B first (highest relevance), rerank A and C
λ=0.5 controls diversity weight. Sign=-1 means destructive (penalty for redundancy). Sign=+1 means constructive (boost for diversity).
QPRP(B) = 0.8901 ← selected first, no adjustment // Rerank A: A and B are similar (Tr=0.4593) → destructive, sign=-1 QPRP(A) = score(q,A) + λ × sign × √(score(q,B)) × Tr(ρ_B · ρ_A) = 0.7547 + 0.5 × (-1) × √0.8901 × 0.4593 = 0.7547 + 0.5 × (-1) × 0.9435 × 0.4593 = 0.7547 - 0.2167 = 0.5380 ← penalized for redundancy with B // Rerank C: B and C are different (Tr=0.0584) → constructive, sign=+1 QPRP(C) = score(q,C) + λ × sign × √(score(q,B)) × Tr(ρ_B · ρ_C) = 0.0236 + 0.5 × (+1) × 0.9435 × 0.0584 = 0.0236 + 0.0276 = 0.0512 ← boosted for diversity from B

Complex Embeddings

What are complex embeddings?
A classical embedding gives each dimension one real number — just a magnitude. A complex embedding gives each dimension a complex number: magnitude × e^{iφ}, where φ (phi) is the phase angle. Magnitude encodes how much of that concept is present. Phase encodes which sense or context of that concept is active. Same word, same magnitude — different phase means different meaning.

Technical Terms

Complex Number — Magnitude and Phase
Any complex number z can be written as z = r · e^{iφ}, where r is the magnitude (how big, always ≥ 0) and φ is the phase angle (which direction in the complex plane). Equivalently: z = r·cos(φ) + r·sin(φ)·i = a + bi.

On a 2D plane (real axis horizontal, imaginary axis vertical): r = distance from origin = √(a²+b²), φ = angle from real axis.

Two numbers can have identical magnitude r but completely different phase φ — they look the same in size but point in different directions. This is what allows complex embeddings to distinguish "apple" (fruit, φ=0) from "apple" (brand, φ=π/2) even when both have the same magnitude on the food dimension.
e^{iφ} — Euler's Formula
e^{iφ} = cos(φ) + i·sin(φ). This maps any angle φ to a point on the unit circle in the complex plane.

Key values you need to know:
— φ = 0: e^{i·0} = cos(0)+i·sin(0) = 1+0i → points along real axis
— φ = π/2: e^{i·π/2} = cos(π/2)+i·sin(π/2) = 0+1i = i → points along imaginary axis (90° rotated)
— φ = π: e^{i·π} = -1+0i → points opposite real axis

Multiplying a magnitude r by e^{iφ} just rotates it by angle φ. The size stays the same — only the direction changes. This rotation is how sense information is encoded: same word "apple", same magnitude, different angle = different sense.
Phase φ in IR — What It Encodes
In complex embeddings, the phase φ of each dimension is learned from training data context. If "apple" appears near food words (juice, fruit, recipe), the food dimension gets φ≈0. If "apple" appears near tech words (iPhone, iOS, MacBook), the food dimension gets φ≈π/2 — same magnitude, 90° rotated in the complex plane.

φ = 0 → word appears in its primary intended sense for this dimension (points along real axis)
φ = π/2 → word appears but in a different sense (points along imaginary axis — orthogonal to the intended sense)

When a query with φ=0 meets a document with φ=π/2 on the same dimension, they are orthogonal. Their inner product on that dimension is imaginary — and after squaring it contributes less to the final score than if they were aligned.

Concrete example — what phase actually is:

Imagine the food dimension as an arrow on a clock face. The length of the arrow = magnitude (how much food content). The direction the arrow points = phase (which sense of food).

Doc A "apple fruit smoothie": food arrow points RIGHT (φ=0, real axis) → fruit sense
Doc B "Apple iPhone review": food arrow points UP (φ=π/2, imag axis) → brand sense
Query "apple juice" (fruit): food arrow points RIGHT (φ=0, real axis) → fruit intent

Both Doc A and Doc B have arrows of nearly the same length (~0.6) on the food dimension. Classical cosine only measures the length — it sees 0.6 for both and gives both a similar score.

Complex embeddings also measure the direction. Query arrow points RIGHT. Doc A arrow points RIGHT — same direction, they multiply cleanly: 0.6 × 0.6 = 0.36 (real contribution, constructive). Doc B arrow points UP — perpendicular to query: 0.6 × 0.6i = 0.36i (imaginary, destructive after squaring).

That direction difference — invisible to classical, fully captured by phase — is what creates the 8× score separation shown in the worked example below.

Complex Inner Product ⟨q|d⟩ = Σₖ q_k* × d_k
The scoring function for complex embeddings. The star (*) means conjugate — flip the sign of the imaginary part: (a+bi)* = a−bi. This conjugation is what makes interference possible.

Multiplication rules:
— real × real = real
— real × imaginary = imaginary
— imaginary × imaginary = −real (i × i = −1)

When you multiply q_k* × d_k: if their phases match (both real), the product is real. If their phases differ by π/2 (one real, one imaginary), the product is imaginary. The sum across all dimensions collects these real and imaginary parts separately — this is where interference happens.
Constructive vs Destructive Interference — Precise Definition
Interference happens when contributions from multiple dimensions are summed before squaring.

Constructive: all dimension contributions point in the same direction in the complex plane (e.g. all real). They add up: 0.5+0i plus 0.5+0i = 1.0+0i. Squaring: 1.0² = 1.0.

Destructive: contributions point in different directions. They split the energy: 0.5+0i plus 0+0.5i = 0.5+0.5i. Squaring: 0.5²+0.5² = 0.5. Same individual magnitudes — but half the score because energy is spread across real and imaginary instead of concentrated in one direction.

The rule is not "imaginary result = destructive". The rule is: misaligned contributions across dimensions reduce the score below its maximum possible value for those magnitudes.
Measurement |⟨q|d⟩|² = real² + imag² — The Final Score
After computing the complex inner product ⟨q|d⟩ = a+bi, the final score is its squared magnitude: a²+b². This always gives a real non-negative number — required for a probability score. This is the quantum measurement postulate: observable outcomes are always real non-negative probabilities.

Note: a²+b² is the same whether b is positive or negative. What matters is how much energy ended up in the real part vs the imaginary part after summing across dimensions. More energy in the real direction → higher score for the same total magnitude.

How They Combine — Complex Vectors Feed Into Density Matrices

Complex embeddings and density matrices are not competing approaches. They stack. Complex embeddings produce richer vectors. Density matrices are built from those vectors using the exact same formula.
